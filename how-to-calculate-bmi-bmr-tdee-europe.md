# How to Calculate Your BMI, BMR and TDEE: A Complete Weight-Loss Guide for Europe

Losing weight in a healthy, sustainable way starts with understanding three numbers: your **BMI** (Body Mass Index), your **BMR** (Basal Metabolic Rate), and your **TDEE** (Total Daily Energy Expenditure). Together, these numbers tell you exactly how many calories your body needs — and how many you should eat if you want to lose weight without starving yourself or losing muscle.

This guide walks through each of these numbers using the metric system (kilograms and centimetres), the standard used across the UK, Ireland, Germany, France, Spain, Italy, the Nordics, and the rest of Europe — so every example below reflects the units you already use.

## What Is BMI, and Why Does the NHS Use It?

Body Mass Index (BMI) is a simple ratio of your weight (in kilograms) to the square of your height (in metres). The formula is:

**BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²**

For example, someone who weighs 78 kg and is 1.75 m tall has a BMI of 78 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 25.5.

The UK's National Health Service (NHS) and the World Health Organization (WHO) both use the following BMI bands for adults:

- **Below 18.5** — Underweight
- **18.5–24.9** — Healthy weight
- **25–29.9** — Overweight
- **30 and above** — Obese

BMI is not a perfect measure — it doesn't distinguish between muscle and fat, so a very muscular athlete may show a "high" BMI despite having low body fat. Still, for the general population, it remains one of the most widely used screening tools in European public health guidance, precisely because it's quick, free, and requires no special equipment.

## BMR: The Calories Your Body Burns at Rest

Your Basal Metabolic Rate is the number of calories your body burns just to keep you alive — breathing, circulating blood, maintaining body temperature — if you did nothing but rest for 24 hours. The most clinically validated formula for estimating BMR is the **Mifflin-St Jeor equation**, which factors in your weight, height, age, and sex:

- **Men:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
- **Women:** BMR = (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

This is why age matters so much in weight-loss planning: metabolism naturally slows with age, which is one reason the same diet that worked in your twenties may not work as well in your forties.

## TDEE: Your Real Daily Calorie Need

BMR only accounts for calories burned at rest. Your **Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE)** adds your actual daily activity on top of that — commuting, walking, exercise, even fidgeting. TDEE is calculated by multiplying your BMR by an activity multiplier:

| Activity Level | Description | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary | Desk job, little to no exercise | BMR × 1.2 |
| Lightly active | Light walking, housework | BMR × 1.375 |
| Moderately active | Exercise 3–5 days/week | BMR × 1.55 |
| Very active | Hard exercise or physical job | BMR × 1.725 |

Your TDEE is the number of calories you'd need to eat daily to **maintain** your current weight. To lose weight, you need to eat fewer calories than this — a **calorie deficit**.

## How Big Should Your Calorie Deficit Be?

A safe, sustainable weight-loss rate — recommended by both the NHS and most European dietetic associations — is **0.5 to 1 kg per week**. Since one kilogram of body fat represents roughly 7,700 calories, this translates to a daily deficit of:

- **~500 kcal/day** for 0.5 kg/week loss
- **~1,000 kcal/day** for 1 kg/week loss

Cutting calories more aggressively than this often backfires: it increases muscle loss, slows your metabolism, and makes the weight far more likely to return once normal eating resumes. Slow, steady loss is not just safer — it's also the approach most likely to stay off long-term.

## Protein, Fat, and Carbohydrates: Getting the Balance Right

Once you know your daily calorie target, the next question is how to split it across macronutrients. A commonly recommended split for weight loss, consistent with EFSA (European Food Safety Authority) dietary reference values, is:

- **Protein:** 1.6–2.2 g per kg of body weight — protects muscle mass during a calorie deficit
- **Fat:** roughly 25–30% of total calories — supports hormone health
- **Carbohydrates:** the remaining calories — your primary energy source for daily activity and exercise

Protein deserves special attention during weight loss: it's the single most important macronutrient for preserving lean muscle while losing fat, and it also keeps you fuller for longer than an equivalent amount of carbohydrate or fat.

## Walking: The Most Underrated Weight-Loss Tool

You don't need a gym membership to create a calorie deficit. Walking briskly for 30–45 minutes a day can burn an extra 150–300 kcal depending on your weight and pace — enough, on its own, to account for a meaningful part of a daily deficit. It's also the form of exercise least likely to trigger injury or burnout, which is exactly why it's sustainable for months rather than weeks.

## Track Consistently, Not Perfectly

Research consistently shows that people who track their weight, food intake, and activity — even imperfectly — lose more weight and keep it off longer than people who don't track at all. You don't need a perfect log every single day; you need a *consistent* one. A quick daily weigh-in, a rough food diary, and a step count are usually enough to keep you honest with yourself and catch small trends before they become setbacks.

## Key Takeaways

- BMI is a quick screening tool used by the NHS and WHO — useful, but not the full picture
- BMR tells you your resting calorie burn; TDEE adds your activity on top
- Aim for a deficit of 500–1,000 kcal/day for a safe 0.5–1 kg weekly loss
- Prioritise protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg) to protect muscle while losing fat
- Daily walking is a simple, sustainable way to boost your calorie deficit
- Consistent tracking — even if imperfect — is one of the strongest predictors of long-term success
