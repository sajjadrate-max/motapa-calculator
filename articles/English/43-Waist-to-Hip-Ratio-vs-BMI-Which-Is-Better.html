Waist-to-Hip Ratio vs BMI: Which Is Better?

BMI is the most widely known weight-screening tool, but it is far from the only one. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) is another simple, low-cost measurement that many health professionals consider a useful complement — and in some cases, a better predictor of certain health risks — because it focuses specifically on where fat is stored rather than just overall body size.

What Is Waist-to-Hip Ratio?

Waist-to-hip ratio is calculated by measuring the circumference of your waist at its narrowest point and the circumference of your hips at their widest point, then dividing the waist measurement by the hip measurement. The result is a simple ratio that reflects your body shape rather than your overall size. A higher ratio means more fat is concentrated around the abdomen relative to the hips.

What WHR Measures That BMI Cannot

BMI treats all weight the same, regardless of where it sits on the body. Two people can have an identical BMI while carrying their weight very differently — one with fat concentrated around the waist and abdomen, the other with more weight around the hips and thighs. Research consistently shows that abdominal fat, sometimes called visceral fat, is more closely linked to cardiovascular and metabolic health risks than fat stored elsewhere on the body. WHR captures this distinction, while BMI does not.

General WHR Ranges

Commonly cited reference ranges suggest a WHR below roughly 0.85 for women and below roughly 0.90 for men is generally considered to carry lower associated health risk, while ratios above these points are often associated with higher risk. These figures can vary slightly between health organizations and should be treated as general guidance rather than strict medical thresholds.

Strengths and Limitations of Each Tool

BMI's biggest strength is its simplicity — it requires only height and weight, making it fast and accessible for population-level screening. Its biggest weakness is that it cannot account for body composition or fat distribution. WHR's strength is that it reflects fat distribution, which many studies link to metabolic risk, but it requires a bit more effort to measure accurately and consistently, and like BMI, it is still an indirect estimate rather than a direct body composition measurement.

Using Both Together

Rather than treating BMI and WHR as competing tools, the most practical approach is to use them together. BMI gives a quick overall picture, while WHR adds important context about where your weight is distributed. Someone with a borderline BMI but a favorable waist-to-hip ratio may have a different risk profile than someone with the same BMI and a higher ratio, and seeing both numbers side by side gives a more complete starting point for a conversation with a healthcare provider.

How to Measure Your Waist-to-Hip Ratio at Home

Stand relaxed and breathe out normally before measuring. Wrap a flexible tape measure around your waist at the narrowest point, usually just above the belly button, and note the measurement. Then measure around the widest part of your hips and buttocks. Divide the waist number by the hip number to get your ratio. For the most consistent results, measure at the same time of day and keep the tape snug but not compressed against the skin.

Why Fat Location Matters for Health

Fat stored around the abdomen, often described as "apple-shaped" fat distribution, tends to surround internal organs more closely than fat stored around the hips and thighs, which is more typical of a "pear-shaped" distribution. This visceral abdominal fat is metabolically active in ways that can negatively influence blood sugar regulation, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels, which is why researchers have paid close attention to waist-focused measurements as a potentially more direct signal of cardiometabolic risk than overall body size alone. This doesn't mean pear-shaped fat distribution carries no risk at all, but the research generally points to abdominal fat as the pattern most worth monitoring closely.

Body shape is influenced by a combination of genetics, sex hormones, and age, meaning not everyone can change their fundamental fat distribution pattern through diet and exercise alone. However, reducing overall body fat through a sustainable calorie deficit, combined with regular strength training, tends to reduce abdominal fat specifically over time even if the body's underlying shape tendency remains the same, which is why a comprehensive approach to weight management remains valuable regardless of your current waist-to-hip ratio.

Other Waist-Based Measurements Worth Knowing

Waist-to-hip ratio isn't the only waist-based tool used alongside BMI. Waist circumference on its own — without dividing by hip measurement — is also commonly used, with general guidance suggesting increased health risk above roughly 102 cm (40 inches) for men and 88 cm (35 inches) for women. Waist-to-height ratio is another increasingly discussed alternative, calculated by dividing waist circumference by height, with a ratio above 0.5 often flagged as a point worth paying attention to. Each of these tools captures a slightly different angle on body shape and fat distribution, and no single one is considered definitively superior — together with BMI, they build a more complete and nuanced picture than any one measurement alone.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Is waist-to-hip ratio more accurate than BMI?

Neither tool is universally 'more accurate' — they measure different things. WHR reflects fat distribution and is often considered a better indicator of abdominal fat-related risk, while BMI gives a quick overall size estimate.

What is a healthy waist-to-hip ratio?

General guidance suggests below about 0.85 for women and below about 0.90 for men is associated with lower risk, though exact thresholds can vary between sources.

Can I have a normal BMI but a high waist-to-hip ratio?

Yes, this is possible and is one of the key reasons health professionals recommend looking at more than one measurement — a normal BMI doesn't rule out higher abdominal fat.

How do I measure my waist correctly?

Measure around the narrowest part of your torso, usually just above the belly button, after breathing out normally, using a flexible tape measure kept snug but not tight.

Should I use WHR instead of BMI?

It's best used alongside BMI rather than as a replacement. Together they provide a more complete picture than either measurement alone.
